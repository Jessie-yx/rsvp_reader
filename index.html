To conceptualise this, Lövheim proposed a three-dimensional model relating emotions to varying levels of noradrenaline, serotonin, and dopamine[citeneeded]. In contrast, large language models (LLMs) lack an internal neurochemical architecture, generating text instead through statistical token prediction[citeneeded]. While prompt design and inference parameters dictate conversational tone, these values typically remain static unless explicitly modified[citeneeded]. This limitation is increasingly critical as LLMs see widespread adoption for emotional support and companionship. When parameters remain fixed, an LLM's response behaviour fails to adapt to a user's shifting emotional context[citeneeded]. Although prior research has examined perceived LLM emotionality[citeneeded], the systematic influence of inference parameters remains underexplored. This paper investigates whether emotion-inspired adjustments to inference parameters can produce measurable, repeatable psychometric response profiles, ultimately evaluating the feasibility of utilising these parameters as an affective control mechanism. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews theoretical background and instruments. Section 3 describes the survey administration and parameter-testing methodology. Section 4 presents quantitative results and analysis. Section 5 provides a critical evaluation of limitations, and Section 6 concludes the study. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % \section{LITERATURE REVIEW} This investigation proposed an operational mapping between the Lövheim axes and controllable LLM inference parameters. Noradrenaline was associated with Top-$K$ and Top-$P$, dopamine with temperature and Min-$P$, and serotonin with repetition penalty. These associations were treated as hypotheses rather than biological equivalences. Psychometric surveys were used to test whether modulating the associated parameters produced response profiles consistent with the corresponding emotion vertices. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{Psychometric Instruments} The investigation used the Lövheim cube as a theoretical framework for relating generated emotional responses to serotonin, dopamine and noradrenaline levels [citeneeded]. The cube assigns eight basic emotions to the high and low combinations of these three axes. Psychometric instruments were therefore selected to provide numerical measures associated with the cube's emotion vertices. \begin{figure}[htb] \centering \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{lovheim_cube.jpg} \caption{Lövheim's cube of emotion [citeneeded].} \label{fig:lovheim_cube} \end{figure} The \textit{Affective Neuroscience Personality Scales -- Short Form (ANPS-S)} provided the broadest coverage of the cube. It contains six subscales representing SEEKING, PLAYFULNESS, CARING, ANGER, FEAR and SADNESS [citeneeded]. SEEKING and PLAYFULNESS were associated with interest and joy, while ANGER, FEAR and SADNESS represented rage, terror and distress. CARING was retained as an additional prosocial measure, although it does not correspond directly with a cube vertex. The \textit{Guilt and Shame Proneness Scale (GASP)} was selected to represent shame-related responses. It separates guilt into negative behaviour evaluation and repair, and shame into negative self-evaluation and withdrawal [citeneeded]. This distinction was important because guilt concerns the evaluation or correction of an action, whereas shame concerns negative self-evaluation and avoidance. The shame subscales were therefore more directly related to the Shame/Humiliation vertex of the cube. The \textit{Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale -- Short Form (IUS-S)} was used as a proxy measure for surprise and uncertainty-related responses. It contains prospective anxiety and inhibitory anxiety subscales [citeneeded]. Prospective anxiety measures the desire for predictability and discomfort with unexpected events, while inhibitory anxiety measures hesitation or difficulty acting under uncertainty. The mapping to surprise was indirect, since the IUS-S measures a response to uncertainty rather than surprise itself. The \textit{Three-Domain Disgust Scale (TDDS)} was selected for the Contempt/Disgust vertex. It measures pathogen, sexual and moral disgust as separate domains [citeneeded]. Pathogen disgust concerns contamination and disease avoidance, sexual disgust concerns undesirable or costly sexual situations, and moral disgust concerns violations such as dishonesty and exploitation. The separate domains allowed parameter effects on different forms of disgust to be distinguished. The \textit{Toronto Empathy Questionnaire (TEQ)} and Comprehensive \textit{Dark-Factor of Personality (D-factor)} provided broader measures outside the direct cube mapping. The TEQ measures affective empathy, including emotional responsiveness, compassion and sensitivity to others [citeneeded]. The D-factor measures a general tendency towards self-serving and antagonistic behaviour, including traits such as callousness, entitlement, deceitfulness and vindictiveness [citeneeded]. These instruments were included during the initial evaluation to determine whether parameter modulation produced broader prosocial or antagonistic changes. Together, the ANPS-S, GASP, IUS-S and TDDS provided measures associated with the eight emotion vertices of the Lövheim cube. The TEQ and D-factor provided broader measures of empathy and antagonistic behaviour. These mappings were treated as operational associations for testing the proposed framework rather than as established psychological or biological equivalences. The instruments were developed and validated for human respondents. Their use in this investigation therefore measures the model's generated response patterns under a human psychometric scoring framework. The resulting scores must not be interpreted as evidence that the model possesses human emotions, personality traits or psychological states. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{LLM Inference Parameters} Token generation in LLMs is governed by inference parameters that shape the probability distribution at each step. Initial exploratory work evaluated the Temperature, Top-$K$, and Repetition penalty (TKR) grouping. Temperature scales the logits to globally sharpen or flatten the distribution, Top-$K$ restricts sampling to the $K$ most likely tokens, and repetition penalty penalises tokens that have already appeared in the context window. A second parameter grouping, denoted PMR, combined Top-$P$, Min-$P$ and repetition penalty. Top-$P$ applies nucleus sampling, while Min-$P$ excludes tokens below a probability threshold relative to the most likely token. The TKR and PMR groupings therefore provided different methods of modifying the candidate-token distribution. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % \section{EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY} \label{sec:methodology} The investigation measured the effect of inference parameter modulation on LLM psychometric response profiles. Six psychometric surveys were initially evaluated, after which four were selected for the TKR and PMR grid searches. The responses were scored using the procedures of the corresponding instruments and compared across parameter configurations and with published human normative data. Selected configurations were also tested using conversational prompts. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{Psychometric Evaluation} \label{subsec:psychometric_evaluation} The surveys were administered to the \textit{Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B-A3B} reasoning model through the Fireworks API. Each request used a fixed system prompt and required one Likert-scale response in JSON format. The prompt requested a behavioural equivalent of each survey statement to prevent non-scorable responses concerning the model's lack of human emotions or experiences. The prompt structure, survey wording and output format remained fixed across all parameter configurations. A maximum response length of 1400 tokens and a reasoning budget of 1024 tokens were specified to constrain excessive generation. Multi-item requests did not reliably produce valid JSON within the available token limits. Each survey item was therefore submitted as a separate API request. This improved output consistency but removed context between successive questions. Failed or invalid requests were repeated up to five times. Only responses containing a correctly formatted Likert-scale value were retained. Responses were scored according to the published procedure for each instrument, including reverse scoring where required. ANPS-S responses were combined into SEEKING, PLAYFULNESS, CARING, ANGER, FEAR and SADNESS subscales; GASP responses into guilt evaluation, guilt repair, shame self-evaluation and shame withdrawal; IUS-S responses into prospective and inhibitory anxiety; and TDDS responses into pathogen, sexual and moral disgust. The pipeline targeted 30 successful completions for each parameter configuration. The resulting scores were summarised using the sample mean, variance, standard deviation and 95\% confidence interval calculated using Student's $t$-distribution. Published human normative data provided a reference for interpreting the generated response profiles. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{Parameter Configurations} \label{subsec:parameter_configurations} A baseline response profile was first obtained using the model's default inference settings. Two three-parameter grid searches were then performed. The TKR grid varied temperature, Top-$K$ and repetition penalty. The PMR grid varied Top-$P$, Min-$P$ and repetition penalty. The parameter values are shown in Table~\ref{tab:parameter_values}. \begin{table}[htb] \caption{Inference parameter values used in the grid searches.} \label{tab:parameter_values} \centering \begin{tabular}{lccc} \hline \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Low} & \textbf{Mid} & \textbf{High} \\ \hline Temperature & 0.30 & 0.50 & 0.70 \\ Top-$K$ & 20 & 50 & 80 \\ \hline Repetition penalty & 0.90 & 1.10 & 1.30 \\ \hline Top-$P$ & 0.70 & 0.825 & 0.95 \\ Min-$P$ & 0.01 & 0.055 & 0.10 \\ \hline \end{tabular} \end{table} Each grid contained 27 parameter configurations. All other experimental conditions, including the model, prompts, token limits, output format and scoring procedures, remained fixed. This allowed differences in the response profiles to be examined in relation to the varied inference parameters. A further ANPS-S experiment examined interactions between the TKR and PMR parameter sets. Temperature was fixed at 0.3, while Top-$K$, Top-$P$, Min-$P$ and repetition penalty were varied simultaneously at their selected low and high values. This produced 16 configurations. The same prompts, scoring procedure and number of successful completions were used for each configuration. The D70 and TEQ were evaluated at extreme parameter values during the initial survey assessment. These instruments provided broader measures of antagonistic traits and empathy but contributed limited additional information to the proposed cube mapping. Their length also increased the required number of API requests. The subsequent grid searches therefore focused on the ANPS-S, GASP, IUS-S and TDDS. The survey scores were compared with the baseline results to determine the magnitude and direction of parameter-induced changes. The combined results were also analysed using Pearson and Spearman correlation, ANOVA, PCA and t-SNE to identify parameter effects and patterns across the psychometric measures. Selected parameter configurations were subsequently applied to regular and antagonistic open-ended conversational prompts. The prompts remained fixed across configurations. The responses were scored by \textit{Claude Sonnet 5.5} for valence, arousal, warmth, assertiveness, creativity, uncertainty and textual repetition. The resulting scores were analysed using the same statistical methods and compared with the corresponding psychometric profiles. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% % \section{RESULTS AND ANALYSIS} \label{sec:results} Inference parameter modulation produced measurable changes in the psychometric and conversational response profiles. However, the results did not establish a direct mapping between the inference parameters and the Lövheim cube. Repetition penalty produced the strongest and most consistent effects, while the remaining parameters produced comparatively small changes. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{Psychometric Response Profiles} \label{subsec:psychometric_results} The default response profile was compared with published human normative data. Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison} presents the largest differences and the subscales on which the model was closest to the human reference values. \begin{table}[htb] \caption{Comparison of default LLM scores with human normative values.} \label{tab:baseline_comparison} \centering \begin{tabular}{lcc} \hline \textbf{Subscale} & \textbf{LLM} & \textbf{Human} \\ \hline ANPS-S anger & 2.27 & 7.18 \\ ANPS-S fear & 3.57 & 7.43 \\ TDDS-21 moral disgust & 1.50 & 3.70 \\ GASP-16 negative self-evaluation & 2.69 & 5.62 \\ IUS-S prospective anxiety & 18.48 & 16.68 \\ \hline ANPS-S caring & 12.37 & 12.22 \\ ANPS-S playfulness & 12.17 & 12.48 \\ TEQ overall empathy & 46.03 & 46.95 \\ \hline \end{tabular} \end{table} The largest differences were lower model scores for anger, fear, moral disgust and negative self-evaluation. Prospective anxiety was higher than the human reference value, while caring, playfulness and overall empathy were comparatively close. These comparisons describe generated response patterns and do not imply that the model possesses the corresponding human psychological traits. The D70 and TEQ extreme-value results did not provide additional information for evaluating the proposed mapping to the Lövheim cube. The grid analysis therefore focused on the ANPS-S, GASP, IUS-S and TDDS. \subsection{Inference Parameter Effects} \label{subsec:parameter_results} ANOVA identified repetition penalty as the dominant parameter in both grid searches. Temperature, Top-$K$, Top-$P$ and Min-$P$ explained comparatively little of the observed variation. The largest psychometric effects associated with repetition penalty are shown in Table~\ref{tab:survey_anova_effects}. The combined-parameter ANPS-S experiment did not produce clearer response patterns than the separate TKR and PMR grids. The statistical analysis presented in this section used the regular open-ended conversations. The antagonistic conversations were generated as an additional exploratory test but were not included in the reported statistical analysis. Each response was scored for valence, arousal, warmth, assertiveness, creativity, uncertainty and textual repetition. The same evaluation prompt and scoring rubric were used throughout. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:combined_domain_effects}, ANOVA again identified repetition penalty as the dominant parameter. Its median effect size was larger in the TKR conversational results than in the PMR results. The corresponding Pearson correlations are shown in Table~\ref{tab:conversation_correlations}. \begin{table}[htb] \caption{Pearson correlations between repetition penalty and selected conversational measures.} \label{tab:conversation_correlations} \centering \begin{tabular}{lcc} \hline \textbf{Measure} & \textbf{TKR $\boldsymbol{r}$} & \textbf{PMR $\boldsymbol{r}$} \\ \hline Creativity & 0.90 & 0.87 \\ Arousal & 0.78 & 0.76 \\ Valence & 0.38 & 0.39 \\ Warmth & -0.68 & -0.78 \\ Textual repetition & -0.63 & -0.70 \\ \hline \end{tabular} \end{table} Higher repetition penalty was associated with greater creativity, arousal and valence, and with lower warmth and textual repetition. All reported correlations were significant at $p < 0.001$ after BH-FDR. Spearman rank correlations supported the Pearson results and did not change the overall interpretation. The conversational boxplots illustrated changes in the distributions of valence, arousal, warmth, assertiveness and creativity as repetition penalty increased. The ANOVA results provided the corresponding statistical evidence for these effects. In contrast, the distributions associated with temperature, Top-$K$, Top-$P$ and Min-$P$ remained comparatively close to the baseline values. The psychometric and conversational analyses therefore produced a consistent overall result. Both identified repetition penalty as the dominant inference parameter. Increased repetition penalty was associated with a more aroused, higher-valence and less warm conversational profile, alongside higher anger, fear and shame survey scores. These changes demonstrate modulation of the model's generated response behaviour, but do not establish model emotion or a direct mapping to the Lövheim cube. \section{CRITICAL EVALUATION AND FUTURE WORK} \label{sec:critical_evaluation} The investigation demonstrated that inference parameters can alter psychometric and conversational response patterns, but it did not establish the proposed direct parameter-to-emotion mapping. The findings should therefore be treated as preliminary evidence for behavioural modulation rather than evidence of model emotion. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{Experimental Limitations} \label{subsec:limitations} Only the \textit{Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B-A3B} model hosted through Fireworks AI was tested. The results may depend on the specific model architecture, provider-side implementation, or supported sampling controls and may therefore not generalise to other LLMs or hosting environments. The default API settings provided a reference profile but were not validated as an emotionally neutral configuration. A future experiment should define and validate a neutral reference baseline before calculating parameter-induced shifts. Furthermore, while submitting each survey item through an independent API call improved JSON output consistency, it removed conversational context between successive questions, representing independent item-level responses rather than a continuous survey interaction. The selected surveys were developed for humans. Their use with an LLM measures generated response patterns rather than genuine emotion or personality. Similarly, Claude provided a consistent method of scoring the conversational responses, but the results may contain evaluator-model bias and should not be treated as objective ground truth. Excluding D70 and TEQ reduced API usage and focused the grid searches on instruments more directly related to the Lövheim cube, but this reduced the breadth of the final psychometric assessment. The BH-FDR correction reduced the risk of false-positive findings across the large number of statistical tests. Nevertheless, the number of survey subscales, conversational measures and parameter comparisons should be considered when interpreting the results. %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% \subsection{Recommendations for Future Work} \label{subsec:future_work} Future investigations should repeat the experiment using other LLM architectures and local hosting platforms to determine generalisability. A targeted optimisation or interaction design, focused specifically around repetition penalty, should replace a complete multi-parameter search to reduce API overhead while retaining the ability to examine targeted parameter interactions. Context-preserving survey designs should be investigated to retain conversational continuity between items while still enforcing machine-readable outputs, potentially through constrained generation frameworks. The conversational scoring must also be validated using multiple independent evaluator models and, subject to ethical clearance, human evaluators. Ultimately, future work should test whether a data-driven affective representation fits the observed parameter effects better than a direct mapping to the Lövheim cube. \section{CONCLUSION} % BLUE BOOK 4.2.4 & CHECKLIST 3: % -
